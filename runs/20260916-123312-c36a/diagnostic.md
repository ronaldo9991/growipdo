## Three biggest gaps

1. No visible account of the regulatory breaches and remediation
   Sarwa's public positioning stresses regulated investing, but the record provided contains no founder-facing explanation of the offers of securities made without approved prospectuses, the resulting penalties, or what changed afterward. This is material because both the FSRA and DFSA acted, the FSRA required an independent review of governance arrangements, and the regulators coordinated their investigations. The account should also state that Sarwa halted the offer and returned investor money after regulatory concerns were raised. Investors, regulators, and enterprise partners will see a mismatch between a compliance-led brand and the absence of a direct account of a significant compliance failure. A credible explanation of accountability, governance changes, and current controls is necessary to assess leadership judgement and regulatory maturity.
   Fix: Publish a plain-language founder statement that acknowledges the breaches, explains the remediation and governance review, and sets out the controls and oversight now in place. Evidence: F1, F2, F3, F4, F5, F49, F55, F57, F60.

2. Headline business metrics lack independent support
   Public messaging includes major claims about client assets, profitability, user growth, funding, and investor backing, yet the findings classify these as company-stated or only partially verified rather than independently confirmed. The material does not provide independently supported financial statements, audited profitability evidence, a methodology for client-assets reporting, or primary confirmation from named investors. A serious investor or enterprise counterparty will discount headline scale claims if they cannot establish what is measured, at what date, and by whom. The gap is especially important for a platform handling client money, where assets, revenue quality, profitability, and custody arrangements affect risk assessment.
   Fix: Release dated, clearly defined operating and financial metrics with independent assurance where possible, alongside primary confirmation of material financing and investor claims. Evidence: F16, F20, F21, F23, F24, F25, F39, F40, F44, F46, F52.

3. Unclear corporate and licensing transition story
   The public record shows that the DIFC entity's regulatory status ended in June 2024, while the ADGM entity remains active and authorised for managing assets and arranging investment deals. Yet the findings do not show a clear founder-led explanation of why the DIFC entity withdrew, how customers and activities were transitioned, or which legal entity and permissions apply to each product. Public descriptions of broad investing, trading, crypto, advisory, and money-management services are largely company statements and are not matched to independently verified permissions in the findings. Clients and partners need to know who contracts with them, which regulator oversees each service, and what protections apply. Ambiguity after a licence withdrawal can raise avoidable concerns about continuity, scope of permissions, and customer safeguards.
   Fix: Create a current entity-and-licensing disclosure that explains the DIFC withdrawal, the ADGM operating model, product-by-product permissions, and the customer protections for each service. Evidence: F15, F29, F35, F41, F62, F63, F65, F66, F70.

## Method

The LinkedIn URL was used only to derive a name; LinkedIn itself was never fetched. 36 public pages were searched and fetched (22 readable), each saved under evidence/. Every claim was checked twice, by two different models over independently retrieved excerpts, and the label was set by fixed rules: verified needs both passes to match a primary source (regulator, official register, official list); partially verified means the only support is the company's own word or the details differ; refused means contradicted, not found, or repeated only by press. Numbers in the Summary and Gaps sections are checked mechanically against the verified and partially verified findings.
